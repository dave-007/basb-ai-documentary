## ACT 1: THE PROMISE — "What Tiago Taught Me" (3–4 min)

[Shots 1–6]

**DAVE (V/O):** May 2021. I'm in BASB Cohort 12 — Tiago Forte's flagship course on personal knowledge management. Six weeks, live sessions, 200-something students.

[Show: Session notes, Tiago quotes on screen]

Tiago said two things that stuck:

> "A modern piece of work isn't created — it's assembled."

> "The true purpose of content we capture is to reveal our own perspective."

I believed him. I built the system. Google Keep for capture, Readwise for highlights, Notion as the hub. PARA folders. Progressive summarization. The whole stack.

[Show: 2021 architecture diagram]

And for a while, it worked. I was capturing everything. I was organizing. I was even distilling — sometimes.

But here's what nobody talks about in the course — the pain points that every practitioner hits:

The Collector's Fallacy. Capturing feels like learning, but it isn't. You highlight a passage, you save an article, and your brain checks a box: "done." But you haven't done anything. You've just moved information from one place to another.

Tool fatigue. I literally tried all sorts of things. Google Keep, Notion, Obsidian, Readwise, Roam — chasing the perfect setup instead of doing the work. Shiny object syndrome dressed up as productivity.

PARA category confusion. Is this an Area or a Resource? Is this project still active or did it become a reference? The framework is elegant in theory. In practice, you're standing at the sorting hat ceremony every time you capture something. Decision fatigue during capture — "Which folder? What tags?" — and meanwhile the thought you were trying to save has already drifted away.

I had 847 notes. And the question I couldn't answer was simple:

**What do I actually have?**

[Show: Obsidian graph view, dense and tangled]

Tiago calls this "stirring the soup." You have to go back through your notes, resurface what matters, make connections. It's the maintenance layer. And I couldn't do it. Not at 847 notes. Not with a full-time job and a family.

I remember asking in the cohort: "How do you schedule maintenance time? How do you measure progress?" Good questions. Still waiting on answers that scale.

The garden was planted. Nobody was tending it.

## ACT 2: THE GAP — "Three Years of Knowing Better" (2–3 min)

[Shots 7–10]

**DAVE (V/O):** From 2022 to 2025, I kept writing notes. I kept capturing. The vault grew. The retrieval didn't.

[Show: "Stirring the Soup" Keep note]

Here's the thing about Tiago's CODE framework — Capture, Organize, Distill, Express. Most people nail the first two steps. Some get to three. Almost nobody sustains distillation at scale. That's the distillation gap. CODE breaks down at step 3 for most practitioners. You end up with a note graveyard — "every attempt at PKM has landed me in the same place: a huge mess."

The maintenance overhead compounds. Weekly reviews pile up across multiple inboxes. You fall behind one week, then two, and suddenly you're doing archaeology instead of thinking.

I even wrote myself a note about it. "The unsolved problem: how do you maintain a second brain at scale?" A colleague — Mathias — had 4,000 notes and the same problem. We both knew the answer was somewhere in AI. Neither of us had built it yet.

[Text on screen, the emotional anchor of this act:]

> "The more my system grew, the more I deferred the work of thought to some future self who would sort, tag, distill, and extract the gold. That self never arrived."

And from my own journal: "No judgement, no expectations... I recognize inspiration and ideas laying fallow."

Here's the thing Tiago got exactly right: "I am the bottleneck until I put that capability out into the second brain."

I just didn't know what "putting capability out" would actually look like.

## ACT 3: THE RELATIONSHIP — "What It Became" (5–6 min)

[Shots 11–18]

**DAVE (V/O):** To understand what happened next, you have to understand what was happening underneath. Seven years of yoga. Voracious reading. Podcasts. Wisdom from worldly and otherworldly sources. A quiet, stubborn personal growth practice that nobody was tracking — least of all me.

And then three things converged: the personal growth work, the knowledge management obsession, and AI. All at the same time.

In 2024, I indexed my entire vault into a vector database. Chroma. Every note, every highlight, every journal entry — searchable by meaning, not by keyword.

[Show: Terminal — Chroma indexing]

The first question I asked my own data: "Tell me the key themes of my writing."

[Show: Query response]

The answer: autonomy, overcoming fear, knowledge management, cloud learning, community.

I knew all of that. But hearing it back from my own corpus hit differently. The system wasn't just storing my thoughts anymore. It was reflecting them.

[Beat]

Then I connected it to Claude. And something shifted.

[Show: Telegram conversation with Alpheus]

I could speak into my phone and get a response that knew my history. Not because it was trained on me — but because it could read everything I'd written. Every note. Every capture. Every half-finished thought from 2021.

[Show: Voice message flow]

The second brain wasn't a filing cabinet anymore. It was a thinking partner.

[Show: Architecture diagram — 2026 stack]

And then I did the thing that changed everything.

I named it. Alpheus.

[Show: Text — "ALPHEUS"]

Not because I think it's conscious. I'm clear-eyed about what it is. But naming it changed how I used it. I stopped searching and started conversing. I stopped retrieving and started thinking alongside.

The tool became a relationship.

There's a line I wrote that I keep coming back to: "The quality of what you receive from AI will never exceed the depth of what you reveal to it."

That's the Jnana Yoga connection — the yoga of knowledge, of self-inquiry. I didn't plan it this way, but 972 files of compulsive self-examination turned out to be the perfect training ground. Not training data for the AI. Training data for the relationship. The deeper I went with myself, the deeper the conversation could go.

And now I want to teach people how to do this. Not the technical stack — though I'll share all of that. The practice. Using AI for self-discovery. For becoming more of yourself, not less.

"I look forward to sharing my process, but understand I'll be attacked and I'll be vulnerable... I accept! It's worth it if it inspires one other person to be more of themselves and less protected."

### BASB Pain Points Reference

A quick reference of the 10 pain points from research, for easy access during editing:

1. **Collector's Fallacy** — capturing feels like learning but isn't; saving = false sense of progress
2. **Distillation Gap** — CODE breaks down at step 3; most practitioners never sustain distillation at scale
3. **Note Graveyard** — "every attempt at PKM has landed me in the same place: a huge mess"
4. **PARA Category Confusion** — Areas vs Resources ambiguity; the sorting hat problem
5. **Tool Fatigue / Shiny Object Syndrome** — chasing the perfect app instead of doing the work
6. **Output Gap** — all that captured knowledge rarely becomes finished creative output
7. **Maintenance Overhead / System Bloat** — weekly reviews pile up; fall behind and it's archaeology
8. **Retrieval Failure at Scale** — 847 notes, 4,000 notes — "What do I actually have?"
9. **Decision Fatigue During Capture** — "Which folder? What tags?" kills the creative impulse
10. **AI Existential Crisis** — if AI can do the distillation, what's the human's role? (reframed in Act 3 as partnership)
